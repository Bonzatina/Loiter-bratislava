# Devín Castle (hrad Devín)

Devín Castle (hrad Devín, officially Devínsky hrad until 1990) is a conserved ruin of a Gothic, later repeatedly rebuilt castle on a crag above the confluence of the Morava and the Danube, overlooking the village of the [[4-devin|Devín borough]]. Together with [[Bratislavsky-hrad|Bratislava Castle]] it forms the pair of key monuments of Slovak national history: Celtic, Roman and Great Moravian worlds met beneath its walls, and in the 19th century the ruins became a pilgrimage site of the national revival. A national cultural monument since 1961 (declared as "Devín — Slavic hillfort"), today it is an open-air exposition of the [[Muzeum-mesta-Bratislavy|Bratislava City Museum]].

![Devín Castle seen from the Devínska Kobyla massif (hrad Devín)](/assets/hrad-devin.jpg)

The Devín rock has been settled almost continuously since the Neolithic (around 5000 BC); archaeology distinguishes four strongest layers — Hallstatt, Celtic, Roman and Great Moravian. Around the turn of the 2nd–1st centuries BC the Celtic hillfort formed a "triangle" with the oppidum on the site of Bratislava and the Braunsberg hillfort near Austrian Hainburg. Under the Romans, Devín joined the Limes Romanus as a forward post opposite Carnuntum — the first masonry buildings on the territory of Slovakia stood here, including a 13.5 × 7 m structure with an apse whose foundations are exposed on the middle castle; the Roman presence ended around AD 400.

In the 9th century a mighty Great Moravian stronghold rose on the site of the Roman fort — a shield against the expansion of the Frankish Empire. Under the name Dowina it is first mentioned in the Annals of Fulda for the year 864, when the East Frankish king Louis the German besieged Prince Rastislav here; the siege ended in negotiations. The excavated foundations of a Great Moravian church from the second half of the 9th century, with three apses in a "trefoil" plan, testify to the spiritual flowering of the era of the mission of Cyril and Methodius, who — by one account — crossed the Danube near Devín.

A stone castle appeared at the turn of the 12th–13th centuries as a border fortress of the Kingdom of Hungary: a hexagonal tower of the upper castle occupied the entire crown of the rock. In 1419 King Sigismund granted Devín to Palatine Nicholas Garay, under whom the fortress became a late-Gothic aristocratic residence with a residential palace. The castle then passed to the Counts of Svätý Jur and Pezinok (1460–1527), and from 1527 to the Báthory family, who gave the middle castle its Renaissance palaces and the famous little tower Mníška (also Panenská veža, the Maiden Tower) on a sheer rock spike — Devín's most photographed silhouette, wrapped in legends. The last Báthory owner, Stephen V, was the elder brother of the "Lady of Čachtice", Elizabeth Báthory. From 1635 until the 20th century Devín belonged to the Pálffy family, who never resided here; in 1809 retreating Napoleonic soldiers blew the fortress up, and it has remained a ruin ever since.

It was the ruins that made Devín a national symbol: in the poetry of Ján Kollár and Ján Hollý it became the cradle of Slavic glory, and on 24 April 1836 the youth of Ľudovít Štúr made their legendary excursion here, swearing loyalty to the nation "on the graves of ancient glory". In 1896 the Hungarian government erected a 22-metre millennium monument to an Árpád warrior on the upper rock — Czechoslovak legionaries blew it up on 21 January 1921. In 1932 the state bought the ruins from the last Pálffy; systematic excavations begun in 1950 continued for about half a century, making Devín one of the longest-studied archaeological sites in Slovakia.

Today's route leads from the north gate through the lower castle (foundations of an early Christian chapel and a 15th-century guardhouse) to the middle castle with the conserved walls of the Garay and Báthory palaces, the foundations of the Roman building and the 60-metre castle well; the viewing terrace opens a panorama of the confluence of the Danube and the Morava — the [[iron-curtain|Iron Curtain]] border ran along the Morava itself, and today the nationwide Path of the Heroes of the SNP (Cesta hrdinov SNP) starts here. Since 2004 the castle hosts a summer festival of fencing, music and crafts. At the foot of the rock survives the [[Amfiteater-Devin|amphitheatre]] — the "people's theatre" of Devín's brief Third Reich chapter.
