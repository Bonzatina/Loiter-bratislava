# Apollo Bridge (Most Apollo)

Apollo Bridge (Most Apollo) is a steel arch over the Danube, ceremonially opened on 4 September 2005: an extension of Košická street (the project's working name was in fact Most Košická), connecting the left bank with Einsteinova in [[5-petrzalka|Petržalka]]. Its final name commemorates the Apollo refinery — the oil-processing plant that stood exactly where the bridge ramp meets the Danube.

The bridge was designed by engineer Miroslav Maťaščík, who later also rebuilt the neighbouring [[Stary-most|Old Bridge]]. The six-span structure with a 231-metre main span (total length 835 metres) hangs on radially arranged cables from two arches inclined towards each other. The bridge became famous for its erection: the main span was assembled on the left bank parallel to the river, one end was set on a spherical bearing with a quarter-circle sliding track, the other was pushed out onto a raft of barges — and over a few days the whole structure was rotated over the Danube into its final position. For this operation the bridge received the prestigious OPAL Award of the American Society of Civil Engineers in 2006 and the title of Slovak "construction of the year".

![Apollo Bridge (Most Apollo)](/assets/most-apollo.jpg)

The Apollo refinery processed mineral oils from 1895 and was one of the city's main industrial enterprises — its legacy next door is kept by the [[Jurkovicova-teplaren|Jurkovič Heating Plant]]. The heaviest chapter of its history was the bombing of 16 June 1944: the Allies, seeking to cut off the fuel supply of the German forces, bombed the plant; many Apollo workers died, as did other townspeople — the bombs also hit residential areas. After the war the destroyed refinery was not rebuilt; today's Slovnaft considers itself its successor.

When choosing a name for the new bridge, it was decided to commemorate the refinery itself: on opening day a memorial plaque by academic sculptor Andrej Margoč, donated by Slovnaft, was unveiled on the bridge. The plaque had two parts, but the lower, inscribed part disappeared in 2011 — thanks to vandals or metal scavengers.
