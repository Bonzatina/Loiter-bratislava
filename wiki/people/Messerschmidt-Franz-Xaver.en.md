# Franz Xaver Messerschmidt

Franz Xaver Messerschmidt (6 February 1736, Wiesensteig — 19 August 1783, Pressburg) was a German-Austrian sculptor world-famous for his "character heads" (Charakterköpfe) — busts with faces contorted into extreme grimaces. He spent the last six years of his life living and working in Pressburg, in a house with a studio in [[Zuckermandel]], — and it was here that he created most of the legendary series.

![Messerschmidt's self-portrait (alabaster, Pressburg, c. 1780)](/assets/messerschmidt-franz-xaver.jpg)

He learned sculpture from his uncles — Johann Baptist Straub of Munich and Philipp Jakob Straub of Graz — and from 1755 at the Vienna Academy; his career took off brilliantly: bronze busts of the imperial couple (1760–1763), large tin statues of Maria Theresa and Francis I (1764–1766) in the spirit of Late Baroque courtly representation, and after a Roman journey in 1765 some of Vienna's first neo-classical works, from the bust of court physician van Swieten to the severe portrait of Franz Anton Mesmer inspired by Roman republican portraiture. Around 1770–1772 he began the character heads — and at the same time his troubles began: in 1774, a candidate for a professorship at the academy where he had taught since 1769, he was instead removed from teaching — Count Kaunitz, in a letter to the Empress, praised his gifts but cited a "confusion in the head". Embittered, the master left — first for his native Wiesensteig and Munich, where he waited two years in vain for promised commissions, and in 1777 for Pressburg, invited by his brother, the sculptor Johann Adam.

A unique account of the Pressburg studio survives: in 1781 the sculptor was visited by the Berlin man of letters Friedrich Nicolai. Messerschmidt explained to his guest that he pinched himself under the right rib, observed the grimaces in a mirror and transferred them into alabaster and metal, having set himself the goal of recording all 64 "canonical grimaces" of the human face; he was a devotee of Hermes Trismegistus and believed that at night he was tormented by the "Spirit of Proportion", jealous of the secret he had unlocked. The people of Pressburg considered the master possessed and said he had sold himself to the devil; tradition has it the priest refused him a proper burial, and the exact place of his grave is unknown. When Messerschmidt died at 47, **69 character heads** were found in his house. What lay behind the series is disputed to this day: from paranoia and hallucinations (Ernst Kris's classic reading) to Crohn's disease and — per the newest research — dystonia, which causes involuntary spasms of the facial and neck muscles.

His posthumous fame became worldwide: the original heads are held above all in Vienna's Belvedere and in private collections (two heads sold at Sotheby's in 2004 for over six million dollars), while Bratislava has only casts, in the Slovak National Gallery and the City Gallery. The master's house perished in the demolition of the Podhradie for the [[Most-SNP|SNP Bridge]], but the new quarter remembers him: [[Zuckermandel]]-2017 has an F. X. Messerschmidt square with seven life-size bronze replicas of the heads.
