# Alois Balán

Alois Balán (2 February 1891, Valašské Meziříčí — 11 May 1960, Brno or Zlín) was a Czech rationalist architect and one of the key figures of interwar Bratislava. In partnership with [[Grossmann-Jiri|Jiří Grossmann]] he spent almost twenty years building Slovakia's new capital — from the [[Budova-YMCA|YMCA building]] to major state works of [[functionalism|functionalism]].

![Alois Balán](/assets/balan-alois.png)

The son of a woodcarving teacher, Balán graduated in architecture from the Czech Technical University in Prague (1914) and in 1919 — among the first Czech architects to do so — opened a studio in Bratislava, which had been booming since the birth of Czechoslovakia. His early work is classical with a cubist note, influenced by Gočár and Janák; the red-brick [[Budova-YMCA|YMCA building]] stands out from this period (by Czech accounts his independent work of 1920, though it is usually attributed to the duo). In 1922 Balán joined forces with the Prague-born Grossmann in a common firm, and their style turned decisively towards rationalism. Within the duo Balán was the "colourful" and monumental one: the feel for colour in the YMCA and the postal houses gave way over the years to powerful cornices, staircases and portals; Grossmann balanced him with calm linearity, and by the end of their collaboration the two hands had merged indistinguishably.

Balán was also co-author of the regulation plans of Bratislava (in the international competition of 1929 his and Grossmann's project took second prize), as well as of Púchov, Malacky and Lučenec. After the creation of the Slovak State in March 1939 he, like most Czechs, was forced to leave — he returned to his home town, taught at the woodworking school and served as city architect; after February 1948 he worked in the territorial planning department in Gottwaldov (Zlín).

His principal Bratislava buildings: the [[Budova-YMCA|YMCA building]] (1920–1921), the Club of Slavic Artists — Umelecká beseda slovenská (with Grossmann, mid-1920s), the schools on Vazovova street that came to house the famous School of Applied Arts (1928–1937), and the District Social Insurance office on Bezručova (1936–1939) — next door to the [[Modry-kostolik|Blue Church]].
