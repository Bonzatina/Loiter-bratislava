# Peter Roller

Peter Roller (b. 7 July 1948, Bratislava) is a Slovak sculptor for whom stone has remained the principal material all his life, and an associate professor at the Academy of Fine Arts and Design in Bratislava. To Bratislavans he is best known for the cascade at the [[Nova-budova-SND|new SND building]], yet his most widely distributed work stands on no square at all — it sits in pockets and on windowsills around the world: the Danube stones he paints.

![Peter Roller](/assets/roller-peter.jpg)

He was born to the press photographer Ladislav Roller and Marta, née Čaplová, the second of three sons. His father shot not only news but the Slovak art scene — artists and their works; after his death in 1965 most of the archive was irretrievably lost in a move. Roller's early childhood fell on what was then Stalin Square — today SNP Square — and from the window the child watched May Day processions, military parades and the films projected onto the wall of the house opposite. He played in the small park by the Stalin monument; at the age of four he urinated on the back of its plinth, was caught by an officer of the Public Security and saved from the consequences by his grandmother. He denies any political motive in the act today, but does not rule out its subconscious influence on his choice of sculpture as a profession.

Roller studied first at the School of Applied Arts (1963–1967) in the woodcarving department under L. Korkoš and A. Drexler, then at the Academy of Fine Arts (1969–1975) in the figurative sculpture department under Ján Kostka, Alexander Trizuljak and Ján Kulich. He has exhibited at home and abroad since 1976, has been a member of the [[Gerulata|Gerulata Association]] since 1988, has taught at the Academy since 1992 and been an associate professor since 1996. He sits on the presidium of the Lindabrunn symposium in Austria, founded the Nippon Club and served as its president in 1990–1998, and has taken part in more than forty international sculpture symposia worldwide — he received a prize for works made at them as early as 1980 in Hungary, the Slovak Union of Visual Arts prize for the sculpture of the Piešťany parks in 1989, and the Martin Benka Prize in 1991. He works in both monumental and chamber sculpture, draws and makes prints; the core stays stone, but steel, stainless steel and models of monumental objects have joined it in recent years.

A line of its own is Petrograms (Petrogramy), a participatory project running for over two decades: Roller marks found stones with his own patterns — peculiar, mysterious signs recalling now a black-and-white keyboard, now musical notation — and gives them away. For the installation at the Slovak National Gallery (2023) he used Danube stones exclusively: some are gathered inside the gallery, others are gradually carried outside for visitors to find and take home. The stones work as talismans with a "sedimented memory" and a tie to the place they were picked up; the set came to the gallery as a gift from Tatra banka. His recent gallery work also includes postcard-sized collages named after railway stations and cities — from Paris and Venice to London — among which Bratislava addresses turn up: [[Metropol|Metropol]], [[Obchodny-dom-Teta|Teta]], Baťa.
