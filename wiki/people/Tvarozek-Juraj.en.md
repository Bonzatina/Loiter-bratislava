# Juraj Tvarožek

Juraj Tvarožek (30 November 1887, Brezová pod Bradlom — 12 January 1966, in the same town) was one of the most significant Slovak architects of the interwar period and the author of the [[Metropol|Metropol]] building, counted among the first important works of Slovak functionalism. He held no university degree: a gymnasium in Bratislava, an industrial school in Budapest, and then practice — masonry work, a year with the Prague architect Alois Dryák (1908) and building practice in Budapest until the First World War. Reference works disagree on the dates of his schooling but agree on the essential point: he learned the profession on site, not in a lecture hall.

After the war and captivity in Russia, Tvarožek returned to Bratislava and worked independently from 1920. His Slovak signature carries motifs of Myjava folk architecture and art — a regional note that set him apart from the dogmatic functionalists. His main works in the city are the [[Metropol|Metropol residential building and cinema]] (1927–1928), financed by his brother Tomáš; first prize in the competition for Bratislava's city plan (1929); the City Savings Bank on SNP Square (1929–1931); the Slávia sports complex project in [[5-petrzalka|Petržalka]] (1932); and the Slovak Grain Company building (1941).

Politics ran through his family twice. In 1939 Tvarožek signed a petition against the dismemberment of Czechoslovakia, he took part in the Slovak National Uprising himself, and his nephews Branislav and Živodar were in the resistance and the 1944 uprising. Under the communists the family was persecuted: several members went to prison, and one of the best-known trials bore the name "Tvarožek a spol." The architect himself was imprisoned in the 1950s, came back with his health broken, and died in his native village, where he is buried.
