# Jiří Grossmann

Jiří Grossmann (4 April 1892, Prague-Smíchov — 9 April 1957, Prague) was a Czech architect and urbanist, the second half of interwar Bratislava's principal architectural duo. Together with [[Balan-Alois|Alois Balán]] he was among the first to bring to the city an architecture freed of superfluous decor, and for decades he thought about how Bratislava should grow.

![Jiří Grossmann](/assets/grossmann-jiri.png)

The son of an engineer at the Prague governor's office, Grossmann took his architecture degree at the Czech Technical University in Prague (1919), starting out under the influence of the "Prague school" of Janák and Gočár. In 1921–1922 he worked in the building department of the Bratislava directorate of the Czechoslovak State Railways, and in 1922 joined forces with [[Balan-Alois|Balán]], already established in the city. Their joint evolution is a synopsis of the era: from decorativism and the search for a "national style", through the transitional building of the railways directorate (1925–1927), to mature [[functionalism|functionalism]], where form and material became the carriers of expression — unplastered brick, concrete, metal and glass, flat roofs. The duo's most intensive period fell on 1928–1932: the Radiojournal palace, the Land Office, the schools on Vazovova (which came to house the School of Applied Arts), the insurance office in Banská Bystrica. Within the duo Grossmann was the calm, linear, balanced hand — a counterweight to Balán's colour and monumentality; by the end of their collaboration the authors are almost impossible to tell apart.

Urbanism is a chapter of its own: Balán wrote on the regulation of Bratislava as early as 1921, Grossmann followed with articles on public gardens, by 1924 they published the first sketch of the city's regulation plan, and in the international competition of 1929 their project took second prize. The concept rested on moving the main station towards Žilina and growing the city eastward over flat terrain, betting on Bratislava as a Danube port and an industrial-commercial hub — ideas that in many ways anticipated the city's actual development.

After the creation of the Slovak State in 1939 Grossmann returned to Prague, where during the occupation he retrained as an acoustics designer (his "Acoustics in Building Practice" came out in 1947), and in his last years painted watercolours. He died in Prague in 1957.
