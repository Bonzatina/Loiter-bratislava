# Štefan Osuský

Štefan Osuský (1889–1973) was a Slovak lawyer, diplomat and politician — the man thanks to whom [[5-petrzalka|Petržalka]] remained with Slovakia after the First World War. Born in Brezová pod Bradlom, at sixteen he was expelled from the Lutheran lyceum for failing to respond when the education minister Count Albert Apponyi promised he would "make a good Hungarian" — and in 1906 he left for the USA, where he studied theology, natural sciences and law and joined the Slovak movement. In 1917 his reports, passed through adviser George Herron, changed President Wilson's view of Austria-Hungary's future: from "autonomy of the peoples" in the spring of 1918, America moved to supporting "the national aspirations of the Czecho-Slovaks" by June.

![Štefan Osuský in 1933](/assets/osusky-stefan.jpg)

As secretary-general of the Czechoslovak delegation at the Paris Peace Conference of 1919–1920, Osuský "fought for every square metre of Slovak land" — it was through his efforts that the bridgehead on the right bank of the Danube went to Czechoslovakia — and on 4 June 1920 he signed the Treaty of Trianon with Hungary, meeting across the table that same Apponyi, now head of the Hungarian delegation. From 1921 he was ambassador in Paris for almost twenty years; in March 1939 he refused to hand the embassy over to the Reich and began organizing resistance abroad, signing the treaty on the re-establishment of the Czechoslovak army in France with premier Daladier. His conflict with the centralist Beneš — Osuský demanded the Slovaks be recognized as a distinct nation — cost him all his posts (1942); he lectured at Oxford, then as a professor at Colgate University, and died on 27 September 1973 in Herndon, Virginia. At home he was remembered late: only in 1991 did Petržalka's Thorez street become [[Mural-Mier|Osuského street]].
