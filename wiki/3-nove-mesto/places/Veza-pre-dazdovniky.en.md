# The Swift Tower (Veža pre dážďovníky)

The Swift Tower (Swift Tower – Veža pre dážďovníky) stands on a raised patch of grass above Limbová street in Kramáre, in front of the policlinic of the Slovak Medical University. It is the first nesting tower for swifts built in Slovakia — a work of a new generation, in which architecture, design and nature conservation turned out to be one and the same job. It was built in 2015–2016 by the architects of the 2021 Architekti studio (Peter Lényi, Andrea Froncová, Ondrej Marko, Marián Lucký, Marek Harčarík, Peter Lipovský) together with the conservationists of BROZ, the Bratislava Regional Conservation Association.

![The Swift Tower on Limbová street (Veža pre dážďovníky)](/assets/veza-pre-dazdovniky.jpg)

The structure is deliberately plain and made to be repeated elsewhere: three ordinary reinforced-concrete posts of the kind used to carry power lines hold up a structural ring four metres across. Small wooden boxes are mounted around the ring in stacks of three, which gives its silhouette a broken, saw-toothed edge. There are 120 boxes, and some of them carry the names of donors, since the tower was largely paid for by a successful crowdfunding campaign. It rises 8.75 metres above the ground, and each nesting box measures 20 × 25 × 35 cm.

The common swift (dážďovník) is protected all year round and spends almost its entire life in the air: it hunts insects, mates and sleeps on the wing. It needs solid shelter only briefly, to lay eggs and raise its young; for that it normally looks to tall buildings, bridges, cornices, gaps under roofs and tree hollows, from which birds of prey — and people, out of ignorance — often drive it away. Swifts are useful insectivores that can dispose of a great many mosquitoes, and in a number of countries towers, nest boxes and special façade openings have long been built for them, as they are for bats; among the best-known examples is the Swift Tower in Oxford's university park (2019), with its large stylized bird silhouettes. Swifts void their droppings in flight, so there is no mess beneath their nesting sites — which is why such towers stand quite happily in residential areas, on squares and in parks.

Several locations were considered; in the end the tower is visible both from the street below and from the forecourt of the L. Dérer hospital and policlinic. The work is in good order and serving its purpose, owned and maintained by BROZ. The example matters more than the tower itself: other works in public space, joining conservationists, the municipality, residents, architects and artists in support of biodiversity, can come about the same way.
