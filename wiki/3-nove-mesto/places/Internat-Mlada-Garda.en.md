# Mladá Garda Dormitory (Internát Mladá Garda)

Mladá garda at Račianska 103 is Bratislava's largest student dormitory and probably the most coherent surviving work of Slovak socialist realism — *sorela*. It was designed by [[Bellus-Emil|Emil Belluš]] together with colleagues from the Slovak University of Technology, where he taught; the drawings are said to have taken him two weeks, in the fever of the first socialist construction campaigns.

![Mladá Garda Dormitory (Internát Mladá Garda)](/assets/mlada-garda.jpg)

The government approved construction in May 1953, 690 student brigades came to the site that summer, the first block took residents before the year was out, and the whole complex opened in 1955, timed to International Students' Day; the register of modern architecture dates the building to that year. The plan is strictly symmetrical, four bent wings; a long frieze of frescoes with builders' scenes runs along the façade, the walls carry coloured sgraffito, and the clock tower over the main block follows the model of Renaissance Spiš bell towers, decorated in the same technique. The decorative vocabulary is assembled from stylised Slovak folk ornament and Renaissance composition rather than the dogmatic classicism usual in *sorela*. For all its Stalinist pomp the building is considered functionally excellent: the wide corridors were conceived as places to argue in, and the house held halls with a stage, a cinema, a radio studio and club rooms — infrastructure for student life, not just beds. The original capacity was 2,400.

Behind the main block lies an extensive sports area, including the only 400-metre athletics track in Bratislava that meets competition standards. Mladá garda's residents have included President Rudolf Schuster, Bratislava's chief architect Štefan Šlachta, [[Lasica-Milan|Milan Lasica]] with Július Satinský, the Olympic athletes Eva Lehotská-Glesková and Mária Mračnová-Faithová, and today the sprinter Ján Volko. The university completed a full reconstruction by 2018: new façades and roofs on every block (carried out under specialist supervision because of the artwork), a renewed canteen and kitchen, refurbished rooms. Capacity dropped to 1,373 places in the process, the price of adding single rooms. The building still holds no national cultural monument status, though heritage specialists argue it deserves one: nothing among Slovak student housing matches its spatial and functional idea.
