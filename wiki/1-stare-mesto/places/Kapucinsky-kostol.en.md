# Capuchin Church of St Stephen (Kapucínsky kostol)

The Church of St Stephen the King with the Capuchin friary stands on Župné námestie, at the northern edge of the old town. It is of interest for its history, for architectural forms rare in the city, and for an interior that directly reflects the order's rule — and it is **the only Capuchin church in all of former Hungary dedicated to this saint**.

![Capuchin Church of St Stephen (Kapucínsky kostol)](/assets/kapucinsky-kostol.jpg)

The Capuchins came from Vienna in July 1676 and for the first thirty-five years served in a chapel on today's Michalská street, preaching besides at the Brothers of Mercy, at the Ursulines and in other parishes — and working selflessly through the plague epidemics. They bought the plot on the eastern side by the castle in 1698 from Baroness Ghillányi, enlarged it greatly a year later with a gift of neighbouring land from Countess Czobor, and the generosity of Eleonora Theresia, Countess de Strattmann and Payerbach, made the church and friary possible (a plaque with a German inscription still hangs in the choir: "Pray for the unworthy foundress Eleonora Theresia. 1709"). The friary's foundation stone was laid on 17 December 1708 and the brothers moved in in 1712; the church's on 20 December 1711, it was finished in 1717, and consecrated on 6 June by the bishop of Nitra Ladislav Adam Erdődy. In 1727 the friary was extended towards Kozia street and the Pálffy gardens, with a library hall added to the sacristy at the expense of Primate Imrich Esterházy.

The ground, however, let them down: the church stood on marshy soil, the foundations gave, and by 1735 the building was close to collapse. The interior is the direct opposite of the baroque churches nearby: white walls, modest columned altars in dark brown covered with walnut graining, enlivened only by lighter inlay and by bases and capitals the colour of ivory. This severity is not poverty but programme: it answers the order's pastoral aims and its effort to lead the faithful to a deepened inner life according to the Gospel — hence too the side chapel for private devotion and the separate confessional rooms. In 1725 a column with a baroque statue of the Immaculata was raised on the open ground before the church; until then, from 1723, it had stood by the neighbouring County House.
