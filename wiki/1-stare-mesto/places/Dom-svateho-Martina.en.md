# St Martin's Cathedral (Dóm sv. Martina)

St Martin's Cathedral (Dóm sv. Martina) is Bratislava's principal Gothic church, the largest in the city and since 2008 the cathedral of the newly created Archdiocese of Bratislava; after the castle it is the capital's second most visited sight. For nearly three centuries, from the 16th to the 19th, Hungarian monarchs were crowned here, and a gilded replica of St Stephen's crown still tops its 85-metre tower.

![St Martin's Cathedral (Dóm sv. Martina)](/assets/katedrala-svateho-martina.jpg)

## Eight Centuries in the Building

The cathedral's site was the heart of the nascent town: archaeologists found an Eneolithic settlement and a Celtic layer beneath it, and in the 11th century a rotunda stood here with a burial ground around it, rebuilt in the 12th century into a chapel of St Martin. In 1221, with the permission of Pope Honorius III, the chapter church moved down from the castle hill — and a Romanesque church of the Holy Saviour rose below (first mentioned in 1234). The town began the Gothic rebuilding in 1311–1314, raising the new walls **around the old church, still in use** — it was demolished only in 1432, when the time came to close the vaults. The net vault of the three-nave hall church is linked to a plan by the Viennese master Hans Puchspaum, author of the vaults of St Stephen's in Vienna; the new church was consecrated on 10 March 1452. The unusually long choir was built after 1459 to plans by Puchspaum's successor Laurenz Spenning under the patronage of provost Juraj Schömberg — the vault bosses still carry the arms of King Matthias Corvinus and his Hungarian, Bohemian and Austrian domains (structurally complete in 1476, consecrated in 1497). The last Gothic touches were the Chapel of St Anne (1514) and the southern vestibule (~1510), attributed to Anton Pilgram, with the church's first Renaissance portal. A peculiarity: the west tower was built into the town fortifications and served as a bastion — which is why the church has no west portal, and the entrances lead from the south and north. A curious episode: at the turn of the 17th century over 95% of the townspeople were Protestant, and in 1619–1621 the church served the Lutherans for two years.

## The Coronation Church

From 1563 to 1830 the cathedral was the chief coronation church of the Kingdom of Hungary: 19 coronations — eleven kings (among them one queen, Maria Theresa) and eight royal consorts. The list of the crowned, painted on the choir wall during the re-Gothicisation, contains curious errors — two of Ferdinand III's wives are missing, and Ferdinand IV's coronation is dated 1646 instead of 1647. The symbol of that era was raised onto the tower in 1765: instead of a cross, the spire was crowned with a gilded model of the Hungarian crown, with a parchment honouring Maria Theresa inside. The present replica is already the second (1846, after a fire caused by the lightning strike of 1833); the crown was last taken down for restoration in 2010.

## Donner's Baroque and the Re-Gothicisation

The 18th century gave the cathedral its most precious art. Archbishop Imrich Esterházy added the Baroque Chapel of St John the Almsgiver in 1729–1732 — for the relics of the Alexandrian patriarch (by tradition a gift of Sultan Bayezid II to Matthias Corvinus, brought to Bratislava after Mohács) and for his own mausoleum. The flower of Central European Baroque worked on it: sculptor Georg Raphael Donner, for whom Bratislava became home for ten years, made both the saint's silver reliquary casket and the kneeling figure of the archbishop himself; the dome fresco by Daniel Gran (1731) is the celebrated imperial fresco painter's only work in Slovakia. Donner also created the grandiose baldachin high altar with the equestrian group of St Martin sharing his cloak with a beggar (1734–1735). But in 1865–1877 the cathedral underwent a radical re-Gothicisation under parish priest Karl Heiller and architect Jozef Lippert: the Baroque furnishings — altars, pulpit, epitaphs — were removed, Donner's altar was dismantled (the bronze St Martin group survived and now stands in the south nave), and the busts from his choir stalls were bought up by engineer [[Lanfranconi-Enea|Enea Lanfranconi]], whence they reached Budapest. In exchange the church received its present neo-Gothic look and 23 stained-glass windows of Viennese and Budapest workmanship — one of Slovakia's richest collections of stained glass.

## What to See Inside

The cathedral's oldest artefact is the Gothic baptismal font of 1409, the town's gift "on the Tuesday before the day of St Vitus". By the Pietà altar stands a sculpture group of 1642 with an otherworldly backstory: legend has it that it was commissioned with money revealed by the ghost of a former mayor, who appeared a hundred times to a girl named Regina Fischer. Through a glass panel in the floor of the south nave you can see remains of the 11th-century cemetery and a corner of an early Gothic structure. On the choir walls are four tombs that survived the purist purges, including the Carrara-marble epitaph of cardinal Péter Pázmány by [[Rigele-Alojz|Alojz Rigele]] (1914). A 20th-century trace is the Calvary altar: in 1917 its reliefs were replaced with "war" scenes featuring a praying Franz Joseph (the monarchy's first "war altar"; removed in 1920), and in 2011 a relic of the last emperor, Blessed Karl I, was placed in the altar. The Treasury (opened in 2006–2008 in the rooms under the tower) holds a 106-cm Gothic monstrance and dozens of liturgical treasures; the chapter library lived here for centuries, its jewel the 12th-century Pray Codex — the oldest written monument of the Hungarian language (its holdings are now in the Slovak National Archive). On the gallery stands the 2010 organ by the German builder Gerald Woehl, with four thousand pipes from 5 millimetres to 10 metres. Of the six historic bells only the giant Wederin (1670, 2,513 kg) survived the First World War — the rest were melted down for cannons; in November 2000 the belfry gained seven bells given by neighbours: Maria Theresia (2,200 kg) from Vienna, Szent Margit from Budapest, John Paul II from Warsaw, St Adalbert from Prague and St Vladimir from Kyiv.

The place's musical memory rivals its coronation record: in 1835 Beethoven's Missa solemnis was performed in the cathedral, and in 1884 Franz Liszt himself conducted his Coronation Mass here — his bust stands in a niche by the stairs of Rudnayovo námestie, behind an ornamental railing bearing the first notes of the "Hosanna". The square itself is the former cathedral cemetery, the only burial ground within the town walls, closed in the 18th century.

## The Underground

Six metres beneath the church lie the crypts — burials here were restricted as early as Maria Theresa's legislation. Visitors enter from the Chapel of St Anne; only the largest part of the underground is open, branching into four corridors totalling nearly 60 metres. Under the Chapel of St John the Almsgiver is the archiepiscopal tomb, where four successive archbishops of Esztergom rest in ceremonial metal coffins adorned with coats of arms, hourglasses and skulls: Imrich Esterházy (the one who crowned Maria Theresa in 1741), Mikuláš Csáky, František Barkóczy and Jozef Batthyány. Under the main altar is the Pálffy family crypt, entered from outside by the northern wall: over 245 years at least twenty people were buried in it, including palatine Ján Pálffy (1663–1751), but today all its coffins are empty. In all, the crypts hold dozens of graves: dignitaries, canons, wealthy burgher sponsors, among them cardinal Pázmány and Jozef Ignác Bajza (1755–1836), author of the first novel written in Slovak; the family tomb of mayor Krištof Spindler the Younger was identified by archaeologists in 2003 by the little coffin of his eighteen-month-old son, buried in 1702.

## Practical Information

- Hours: the crypt is open year-round (in the All Souls' week of 1–8 November worshippers have priority)
- Tickets: the crypt is free; the combined-route ticket (choir gallery and the Treasury, Klenotnica) is sold at the information centre on the second floor of the Martineum on Rudnayovo námestie — a burgher house connected to the cathedral, restored by the end of 2021
