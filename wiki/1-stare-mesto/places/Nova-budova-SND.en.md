# New SND Building (Nová budova SND)

The new building of the Slovak National Theatre (Nová budova SND) on Pribinova Street by the Danube is the country's largest theatre complex and one of the landmarks of the new centre: for the first time all three SND companies — opera, ballet and drama — came together under one roof. The road from the first idea to the cut ribbon took some twenty-seven years.

![New SND Building (Nová budova SND)](/assets/nova-budova-snd.jpg)

The first proposals for a new theatre complex appeared as early as 1959, and the plan was approved a year later; competitions for the site and for the architecture followed. The winning design was made in 1980 by the architects Martin Kusý, Pavol Paňák and Peter Bauer, beating 53 rival entries. Construction began in April 1986 and outlived the change of era, arguments over funding and an attempt to hand completion and operation to private hands: a memorandum between the government and a private company was signed in 2004 and cancelled a year later. The ceremonial opening took place on 14 April 2007, the ribbon cut in the presence of the then president Ivan Gašparovič and the culture minister Marek Maďarič. Almost half a century had passed since the first deliberations of the late 1950s.

The composition is two main masses set apart so that they form a semicircular "embrace" turned towards the visitor; the authors meant it as a gesture of the theatre's openness to the city, and a small square arose where the axes cross. The architecture is usually placed with postmodernism, with a technicist note: emphatic horizontal articulation of the facades, glazed planes and light stone — Tatra, or more precisely Spiš, travertine — were meant to give the impression of an industrial structure, a "factory for making art". The scale matches: seven storeys, more than two thousand rooms, about 46,776 m² of floor area and over 262,000 m³ of built volume, with a twelve-storey stage tower rising above the opera stage. The halls are turned at roughly 45 degrees and surrounded by the many rooms and corridors without which a large theatre does not work: rehearsal rooms, dressing rooms and service spaces, public areas, a restaurant, a club, an espresso bar and a canteen. The parking is not small either — 116 places underground, 211 outside and eight for coaches.

There are three auditoriums. The largest is the opera and ballet hall with 901 seats; the drama hall holds 635, and the centrally placed Studio 192 — about 1,700 seats in all. They differ in materials too: drama works with pink seats and red Indian sandstone, the opera with red seats, light travertine and pronounced arched elements on the ceiling, while the Studio is kept largely in black. Daylight plays a large part: glazed foyers tie the interior to the embankment, and the central circulation between the two main parts of the building is lit through a long semicircular skylight. The complex is rich in artworks as well — a fountain with sculpture in front of the building (Alexander Biľkovič, Iľja Skoček, Pavol Bauer), a cascade by [[Roller-Peter|Peter Roller]], a pair of towers by Peter Bauer, and a fountain in the entrance hall by Peter Bauer and the painter Dušan Buřil; exhibitions and theatre costumes are shown in the interiors, so there is a reason to come in even without a performance. By the time the theatre opened, Pribinova and the embankment had turned into a new business quarter — and the building became one of its anchor points.
