# Primatial Palace (Primaciálny palác)

The Primatial Palace (Primaciálny palác) on the square of the same name is the former residence of the Archbishop of Esztergom and the foremost monument of classicism in Bratislava; today the city's mayor (primátor) sits here, and part of the rooms house the picture gallery of the Bratislava City Gallery. The two-storey, four-winged building — a broadly spread, almost symmetrical main front with a projecting bay, a balcony on four Doric columns and a massive triangular pediment — closes the whole southern side of the square.

![Primatial Palace (Primaciálny palác)](/assets/primacialny-palac.jpg)

The site was central even in the medieval town: archaeology traces settlement back to Roman times, and the Archbishop of Esztergom's estate house, with a chapel of St Ladislaus, already stood here under Archbishop Dionýz Szécsi (1440–1465). After Mohács (1526) the court and the church province moved north to Trnava and Pressburg, and under Archbishop Pál Várday the residential parts were rebuilt as a renaissance curia; Juraj Szécsényi added a storey in 1691, and Imrich Esterházy gave it a baroque appearance in 1721. The present building is the result of a radical rebuilding: Cardinal and Primate of Hungary Jozef Batthyány chose to build his residence not in Turkish-ravaged Esztergom but in Pressburg, and in 1774 commissioned his Viennese court architect Melchior Hefele (1716–1794), a pupil of Johann Balthasar Neumann. The contract is dated 24 February 1777, the final variant was approved by the primate on 8 June 1778; the old curia was demolished, and by 1781 the palace stood.

The roof is crowned by Batthyány's huge coat of arms under a prince's cap and a cardinal's hat, and the attic carries — a rarity in the city — six colossal allegorical statues of Sankt Margarethen sandstone: Minerva instructing a youth, Wisdom, Love of the Fatherland, Silence, Purity of Heart and Divine Wisdom. They were carved in 1779–1780 by the Viennese Philipp Jakob Prokopp (the outer groups) and Matthäus Kögler (the four separate figures of the Virtues, and the coat of arms itself); the four decorative vases are by Johann A. Messerschmidt, brother of the famous [[Messerschmidt-Franz-Xaver|Franz Xaver]]. Above the balcony two putti hold the gilded letters C and I — Clementia and Iustitia, the archbishop's motto. The pediment is filled by a monochrome mosaic by Ernest Zmeták: in 1959 it replaced a fresco by Franz Anton Maulbertsch that the weather had all but destroyed during the 19th century (in 1937 [[Rigele-Alojz|Alojz Rigele]], while restoring the attic sculpture, managed to sketch its remains in colour, against a possible restoration). The mosaic repeats the original composition; only the Hungarian coat of arms at the top gave way to a stylised city emblem.

Inside, the ceremonial staircase leads to the piano nobile and the Hall of Mirrors — the heart of the palace, the height of both upper floors, with a balustraded balcony running all the way round. Here, on 26 December 1805, the Austrian prince Johann Joseph Liechtenstein and the French minister Talleyrand signed the Peace of Pressburg, sealing Napoleon's victory at Austerlitz (two marble tablets in the palace passage, in Slovak and German, recall it); on 11 April 1848 Ferdinand V signed the March Laws here, and on 3 August 1968 the Bratislava Declaration was signed — which did not, in the event, avert the Warsaw Pact invasion. Today the hall hosts the city council, concerts and weddings. The chief artistic treasure of the display is a set of six wall tapestries of the first half of the 17th century, on a mythological subject, in five salons of the northern wing: they were found by chance in a heating opening in the anteroom of the Hall of Mirrors during reconstruction work at the beginning of the 20th century, and why they were hidden and how they reached Bratislava remains a mystery. The chapel of St Ladislaus, consecrated on 9 November 1781, is almost illegible from outside: no turret, no accent on the facade, only a red marble relief of the Madonna above the courtyard entrance; inside is an oval dome with Maulbertsch's monumental fresco The Miracle of St Ladislaus (1780). In the courtyard stands a baroque sculptural group of St George with a three-headed dragon: the city received it as a gift from the merchant Rudolf Wahl in 1929 in a wretched state, and owes its present appearance to Rigele, who restored it; the group was set up in the courtyard in 1930.
