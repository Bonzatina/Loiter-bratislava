# Plague Column on Rybné námestie (Morový stĺp na Rybnom námestí)

The plague column on Rybné námestie (Morový stĺp na Rybnom námestí) is a baroque column of the Holy Trinity of 1713, standing where Rybné meets [[Hviezdoslavovo-namestie|Hviezdoslav Square]], raised on money collected from the townspeople in memory of the end of the plague epidemic. The epidemic struck the city at the end of 1712: according to contemporary documents, 3,860 people in Bratislava died of it and another 1,561 fell ill.

![Plague Column on Rybné námestie (Morový stĺp na Rybnom námestí)](/assets/morovy-stlp-rybne-namestie.jpg)

Much of the credit for fighting the epidemic went to the Archbishop of Esztergom and Primate of Hungary, Cardinal Christian August of Saxony — he was both the initiator and the donor of the column, and his name is immortalised on the pedestal. The author of the column itself is unknown; the stone enclosure was made by the little-known Austrian sculptor Martin Victor. By the middle of the 18th century the column had decayed so far that the city council decided to have it repaired, and around 1760 the work was entrusted to the Bratislava sculptor Jozef Sartory. Sartory took the task not as a scrupulous renovation preserving the monument's original values but rather as a free reworking — what can be admired today is the result of his work.

The structure runs like this: a superstructure of circular section stands on a three-sided pedestal. The sides of the pedestal carry reliefs on plague themes — the Last Rites, Saint Rosalia and the Burial of the Dead; apart from the recumbent Rosalia, the other two are cruel depictions of what were almost everyday scenes during the infection. The reliefs are Sartory's work; whether the original column had any relief programme he might have worked from is not known. On the cornice of the pedestal stand figures of Saints Roch, Andrew and Charles Borromeo, and at the top of the column, above a capital with volutes, sits a sculptural group of the Holy Trinity. A balustrade enclosure with a wrought-iron gate surrounds the column, and on separate pedestals within it — also by Sartory — stand the Immaculate Virgin and Stephen I, the first Hungarian king, offering Mary the Hungarian crown lying on a cushion in his hands; the same motif is known from a painting of 1737 on the main altar of the Capuchin Church of St Stephen. The rich ornament, the modelling of the forms and the painterly look of these reliefs and figures already lean towards the coming rococo.

The column was renewed in 1772, 1872, 1925, 1956 and 1980, and a full restoration fell in 2003–2007: it was carried out by the VILLARD association, with the academic sculptor Vladimír Višváder working on it, at a total cost of €175,196. By the time work began, movement in the unstable subsoil had opened the joints of the balustrade, tilted the pedestal and the figure of the Immaculata and torn the paving inside the enclosure; the stone was cracked in places, the inscriptions illegible, the limestone covered with carbon deposits, moss and lichen, and the attributes of Saints Charles Borromeo and Roch were missing. The worst damage was precisely on the original baroque and rococo parts — the shaft with its pedestal and reliefs. After an engineering-geological survey of the foundation the column was secured structurally, then the stone was cleaned, carbon deposits and microflora removed, the limestone consolidated and the lost mass made good; casts were taken of the statues, and after remounting a final conservation and hydrophobisation followed (Keim Lotexan on a silicate base). When the foundation joint was opened — a trial pit to establish how the column was founded — bones were found; the find was reported to the Regional Monuments Board.
