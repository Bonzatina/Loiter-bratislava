# Clarissine Church (Kostol klarisiek)

The Church of the Exaltation of the Holy Cross with the Clarissine convent (commonly just "Klarisky") is a Gothic-renaissance ensemble in the city's historic zone and one of Slovakia's foremost architectural works of those periods. It is also the most many-purposed of Bratislava's churches: in its lifetime the building has been a place of worship, a hospital, a poorhouse, a law academy, a grammar school, an exhibition space and a concert hall. The University Library occupies the former convent today.

![Clarissine Church (Kostol klarisiek)](/assets/kostol-klarisiek.jpg)

The community appeared in Pressburg at the end of the 13th century: Pope Boniface VII confirmed it in 1297, and King Andrew III gave the nuns the abandoned Cistercian convent by the western walls, destroyed by Přemysl Otakar II's troops in 1271 and 1273. The site is ancient: an archaeological survey in 2007 confirmed that a Celtic oppidum with a fortified acropolis stood here as early as the 1st century BC. The church began with perimeter walls of the early 14th century; after mid-century the nave was lengthened, a triumphal arch and presbytery were built, and around 1370 the nave was closed with a cross vault.

**The chief treasure is the tower.** Its construction stretched into the following century, and it was not raised from the ground but built up on the strengthened piers of the nave's south-western corner: a five-sided prism — a rarity in Gothic building — regarded, in form and in artistic treatment alike, as a jewel of the style in the history of Slovak architecture. Gothic sculptures stood on its corbels; four survive — the Madonna and Child, a kneeling king, a black king and St Elizabeth (those outside today are copies). After the earthquake of 1700 the tower had to be repaired, and in 1702 the abbess Eva Barbora Balassa gave it the baroque onion finish.

There was no shortage of misfortune besides earthquakes: a fire from Ventúrska street in 1515 damaged the vault; after Mohács (1526) the nuns fled to Trnava and their convent became a hospital, then a poorhouse; in 1590 it burned again and stood empty for a long time. The ensemble was revived in the 17th century by [[Primacialny-palac|the Archbishop of Esztergom]] Peter Pázmány, who made Pressburg one of the centres of the Counter-Reformation: from 1637 the convent was renewed to plans by Jakub Rava, and the present two-storey late renaissance building with its sumptuous courtyard portal is the result. Joseph II ended the monastic story: the order was dissolved in 1782, the nuns left, and in 1784 the law academy moved into the empty buildings from Trnava, joined from 1786 by a Catholic grammar school — the one where the composer Béla Bartók and the Slovak priest and inventor Jozef Murgaš studied.

Of the original furnishing, three altars survive in the sanctuary with altarpieces by František Anton Palko, and a richly decorated rococo pulpit by Jozef Sartory on the northern wall of the nave — during restoration a parchment with an inscription of 1761 was found inside it. The convent's most distinguished resident was the Bohemian queen dowager Sophia of Bavaria: after the death of her husband Wenceslaus IV she took refuge here in 1419 under the protection of her brother-in-law, King Sigismund of Hungary, lived until September 1425 and is buried in the neighbouring [[Dom-svateho-Martina|St Martin's Cathedral]].
