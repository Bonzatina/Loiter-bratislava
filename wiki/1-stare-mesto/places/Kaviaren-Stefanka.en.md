# Café Štefánka (Kaviareň Štefánka)

Štefánka, on the corner of Palisády and Štefánikova streets, is Bratislava's cult café, for over a century the drawing room of the city's intelligentsia: literature, politics and plain life were argued over here, and the poet Ján Smrek first read aloud the opening verses of his poetic symphony The Poet and the Woman at these very tables. The building was erected in 1897 by Alexander Feigler, and the café's name — Café Stefanie, Štefánia kávéház — honours [[Stefania-Belgicka|Princess Stéphanie]], widow of Crown Prince Rudolf.

![Štefánka on the corner of Palisády and Štefánikova](/assets/kaviaren-stefanka.jpg)

Its lustre came from the Hackenberger dynasty: Béla I became the café's tenant and operator in 1904, and the years 1932–1945 under Béla II are considered its golden age. The family memory lives on — in 2024, for the 120th anniversary of the Hackenbergers' accession "to the throne of Café Stefanie", the café staged an evening of five "stations" based on Béla II's memoirs, with a "café orchestra", Smrek's verses and gourmet specialities; the programme was advised by the dynasty's descendant Adalbert (Béla III) Hackenberger.

Štefánka is a surviving fragment of a whole world: connoisseurs of the old colour counted up to seven hundred cafés and confectioneries in the centre alone, and café life reached its peak in the interwar era. The café was an institution: a proper head waiter knew which newspaper each guest read, the postman brought pensioners their pensions right to the table, lawyers received clients in the café (the better establishments paged them over a loudspeaker), and the "café parasites" ingeniously sent their bill to acquaintances with a little note. The true revellers arrived after eight in the evening — and the night crowd held out till morning.
