# Pisztory Palace (Pisztoryho palác)

The Pisztory Palace (Pisztoryho palác, spelled Pistoriho palác in the borough's own usage) at Štefánikova 25 is an eclectic building of the 1890s and a national cultural monument, number 101-166/0. Among Bratislava's palaces it is a rarity: you can simply walk in. The building belongs to Staré Mesto and works as the borough's cultural centre, with the Film Europe art cinema, exhibitions, theatre and workshops.

![Pisztory Palace (Pisztoryho palác)](/assets/pistoriho-palac.jpg)

The palace was built in the 1890s by Felix Pisztory, a pharmacist of Jewish origin, as his own house and pharmacy. Štefánikova was then only becoming a grand street leading to the [[Hlavna-stanica|station]] of the Royal Hungarian Railway, and well-off Pressburgers were buying up plots along it for palaces and villas — the same wave that had produced the neighbouring [[Karacsonyiho-palac|Karáčoni Palace]] a decade earlier. Behind the house Pisztory kept a garden of medicinal herbs that stretched as far as today's [[Slavin|Slavín]]. In the historian Tivadar Ortvay's book the palace is noted for its taste and luxury, and much of the fitting-out has survived: wrought-iron gates, artistic railings and balustrade, crystal chandeliers, the mosaic floor of the staircase hall, rich stucco. The gilded ceiling paintings — among them an allegory, an apotheosis of music, made in 1895 by a painter the sources name as Gastach — came to light very late indeed: they were found during research by the city's monuments institute only in the late 2000s.

The twentieth century left a layer in the palace that now looks like an exhibit in its own right. Under socialism the building housed the Museum of Vladimir Ilyich Lenin, and several marble tablets survive on the ground floor: two bearing the state emblems of Czechoslovakia and the USSR, two more carved with quotations about the proletariat and communism ("…a mortal hatred of slavery and oppression, a revolutionary ardour that moves mountains, a boundless faith in the creative powers of the masses…"). After 1989 the side wing was occupied by the House of Slovaks Abroad, until 2005; then the building was left to decay for years.

The return began in 2011, when Staré Mesto, together with the civic association VIA CULTURA, Inštitút pre kultúrnu politiku, set about turning the palace into a cultural centre. Reconstruction ran from 2015 and finished in April 2017, paid for by grants from Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway through the EEA Financial Mechanism and by the Slovak state budget. Since 2013 the house has held the Film Europe art cinema — European films, retrospectives and festivals such as Scandi and Be2Can; the Theatre Without a Home (Divadlo bez domova) plays here too, alongside children's programmes, concerts, book launches and exhibitions of painting, sculpture and ceramics. The courtyard was the last part to be put right: in the first half of 2026, to a design by Studio 2021 Architekti, the historic granite setts were restored, a rainwater retention system and shading installed, one more tree planted and sculptures by Matej Rosmány placed — the yard was due to open by early summer.
