# Sándor Petőfi Monument (Pomník Sándora Petőfiho)

The monument to the Hungarian poet and revolutionary Sándor Petőfi (Pomník Sándora Petőfiho) by Béla Radnai stands today in the [[Medicka-zahrada|Medical Garden]] — the fourth place it has occupied in its life. It is the one surviving city sculpture of those that reminded Pressburg of its Hungarian past: its contemporary, the statue of Maria Theresa, was smashed in 1921.

![Sándor Petőfi Monument (Pomník Sándora Petőfiho)](/assets/pomnik-sandora-petofiho.jpg)

The idea belonged to the German journalist Riepl Wolfgang, editor of the newspaper "Westungarische Grenzbote": for the fiftieth anniversary of the poet's death he proposed that a Petőfi statue be made in Bratislava. The statue committee founded in 1899 wanted it made by the city's native son János Fadrusz — author of that very beautiful statue of Maria Theresa of 1897; preparing a Petőfi memorial was a great dream of his own, and in 1902 he informed the city that he would prepare and erect the sculpture at his own expense. A year later the outstanding Hungarian sculptor died. Another year on, the committee announced a public competition open to Hungarian artists only; it was won by Béla Radnai, a student of Fadrusz. The townspeople launched a great public subscription: regardless of nationality, local newspapers, institutions, poorer and richer, workers and the intelligentsia gathered a sum splendid for the time — 75,000 crowns.

Room for it was made in 1904 by shifting the [[Pomnik-Hummela|Hummel monument]] to a nearby small park. The ceremonial unveiling took place on 8 September 1911 on what was then Kossuth Square — today's [[Hviezdoslavovo-namestie|Hviezdoslav Square]] — opposite the [[Historicka-budova-SND|building of the Slovak National Theatre]]. A delegation of the Petőfi Society from Budapest attended, led by the writer Ferenc Herczeg, who gave the address. It stood for less than a decade: after the First World War, under the Treaty of Trianon, Bratislava passed to Czechoslovakia, and the marks of the Austro-Hungarian era began to disappear from the streets — in 1921 the statue of Maria Theresa was smashed. Petőfi was saved: first boarded up, then, by decision of the city council, taken apart and packed piece by piece into crates that were put away in the garage of today's [[Grasalkovicov-palac|Presidential (Grassalkovich) Palace]], and in 1928 moved to a stable.

The sculpture was found only in 1955, in a Bratislava warehouse. The city national committee allowed it to be put up again — on the Petržalka bank of the Danube, which was done in 1956: the spot in the park proved a very beautiful one. But in the sixties the park was named after the Slovak poet Janko Kráľ and his statue was placed opposite Petőfi's — there was no longer room there for Radnai's work, and it was moved to another Petržalka park, to an unsuitable spot beside a public lavatory. For several decades vandals damaged it: painted it, wrecked it, broke pieces off — by the start of the restoration the poet's hand with its sabre, the muse's hand and the palm branch were missing, the stone was cracked, covered with carbon deposits and microflora, in places overgrown with small self-seeded greenery, and marked over with inscriptions and spray paint.

In 2002 the PAMING organisation carried out the restoration of the monument and installed it in the Medical Garden afterwards. The work was done in 2002–2003 by restorers of the VILLARD association under Professor Jozef Porubovič and the academic sculptor Vladimír Višváder; it cost about €60,000, of which €6,600 came from the Pro Slovakia fund and the rest was provided by the Slovak and Hungarian sides under the intergovernmental agreement on cultural cooperation — through the Office for Hungarians Abroad and a collection by the civic association Committee for the Sándor Petőfi Monument. The monument was ceremonially unveiled on 15 March 2003, for the 180th anniversary of the poet's birth.
