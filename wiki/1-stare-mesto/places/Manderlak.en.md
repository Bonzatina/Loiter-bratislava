# Manderlák

The Manderla apartment and commercial block (Obchodný a obytný dom Manderla), known around town simply as "Manderlák", is **Slovakia's first true high-rise**: a 45-metre, eleven-storey tower on the south-eastern edge of SNP Square (Námestie SNP 23). It was commissioned by the celebrated butcher Rudolf Manderla (1884–1968), who conceived "his skyscraper" in 1933 under the impression of the Chicago World's Fair; the design is by architects [[Ludwig-Christian|Christian Ludwig]], Augustín Danielis and Emerich Spitzer, and the daring reinforced-concrete skeleton was built by Pittel & Brausewetter — Central Europe's pioneer of concrete construction, which also erected the city's industrial giants of the turn of the century: the Dynamit Nobel works, Apollo, Cvernovka and the Stollwerck chocolate factory. The building was completed in 1935 (1937 by other accounts) and was the tallest residential building in the country at the time.

![Manderlák](/assets/manderlak.jpg)

The city greeted the tower with hostility: the councillors feared it would "wreck the square" and complicate the crossroads, the traders of the neighbouring [[Stara-trznica|Bread Market]] feared losing their pitches to the "opacha" (monster), and Manderla was even suspected of bribing the magistrate. Structurally the frame runs on eight axes, narrowing to four above the fifth floor — hence the stepped silhouette; facades with colour-accented continuous pilasters and vertical window bands emphasize the height.

On 6 May 1935 Manderla opened on the ground floor the most modern meat shop in Central Europe — with service in three languages, a separate cash desk and rows of counters ("even Vienna had nothing so fine", his son Fritz recalled). One floor up worked the legendary Grand Café, an informal cultural centre of the city: the painter Janko Alexy was a regular, the poet E. B. Lukáč lingered here, the actors Milan Lasica, Ondrej Jariabek and František Dibarbora lived upstairs (Lasica's parents owned the café for a time), and the writer Ľudo Ondrejov "kept the mood going" with tales invented on the spot. The flats were elite: a two-room one cost 180 crowns a month — serious money. The butcher's shop under Manderlák lasted until 2008; in September 2023 the icon of modernism was at last declared a national cultural monument.
