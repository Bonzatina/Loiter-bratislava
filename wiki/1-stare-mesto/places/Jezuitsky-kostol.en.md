# Jesuit Church (Jezuitský kostol)

The Church of the Most Holy Saviour (commonly the Jesuit Church) stands hard against the [[Stara-radnica|Old Town Hall]] — and it is perhaps the city's clearest illustration of the rules of the game in the 17th century: **the church was built as a Protestant house of prayer, and the regulations of the day forbade it to look like a church.**

![Jesuit Church (Jezuitský kostol)](/assets/jezuitsky-kostol.jpg)

The growing number of German Lutherans forced the town to build them a house of prayer in 1636–1638; the German master Hans Stoss of Augsburg raised it in a late renaissance manner on the site of the so-called Armbruster house. The rules were strict: the building must not resemble a sacred structure, must do without a tower, and the presbytery had to form part of the main plan. The resulting church stood out from its neighbours by sheer size — and was consecrated to the Holy Trinity. The Lutherans did not keep it long: in 1672, after the Protestant conspiracy was crushed, it was taken from them and handed to the Jesuits on 1 January the following year. They first dedicated it to St Margaret, then transferred to it the patrocinium of the Most Holy Saviour from the [[Dom-svateho-Martina|cathedral]], rebuilt the interior and set a stucco relief of the order's emblem above the portal — the exterior barely changed, and the church still has no tower.

Inside, the baroque age rewrote nearly everything: the side galleries and the original pulpit were removed, and in 1722 a new high altar was installed by the Viennese joiner Johann Jakob Müller, with an altarpiece by Anton Wellser and sculptures by Johann Andreas Englauer. In a niche by the entrance stands a plaster statue of St Anthony of Padua of 1912 by [[Rigele-Alojz|Alojz Rigele]]. The church's own fate went on zigzagging: after Joseph II dissolved the order in 1773 it passed to the town and served as the court church of the Hungarian governor Albert of Saxony; it was returned to the Jesuits in 1855, and taken away again by the state in the 1950s.
