# Vydrica

Vydrica is the legendary quarter of the Podhradie between the castle hill and the Danube — the "Bratislava Montmartre": for centuries fishermen, artisans and artists mixed here with the red-light district. In the first half of the 20th century Vydrica lived differently from the rest of the city: as contemporaries recalled, "a red light burned above every second doorway", and in the lanes poverty rubbed shoulders with taverns and noise until morning. After the 1922 law abolished regulated prostitution and brothels, most establishments simply "changed their signboards" to hotels and cafés; the police kept secret registers and staged raids (in July 1926, 160 women were detained in two nights) but could not break the pattern — Vydrica remained the city folklore's synonym for sin and squalor.

![The castle and Podhradie with Vydrica from the Danube bank, historic view](/assets/vydrica.jpg)

Vydrica, where the "priestesses of love" had settled as early as the 18th century, left a distinct trace in literature. The Czech poet Josef Rybák, arriving in 1922, honestly described the shock of his first encounter with the "dirty, bedraggled misery of the demi-monde"; the painter Štefan Bednár in 1938 compared the quarter to a port town, and the novelist Dobroslav Chrobák saw "big, lazy, hungry lice" in its dark alleys. But the surrealist generation of the 1930s — Fabry, Bunčák, Rak, Reisel, Žáry — was the first to discover the "poetic nests and nooks below the castle" as a source of inspiration and bold erotic imagery; the locals, as Ľuboš Jurík later noted, "understood sin" and considered the brothel At the Blue Pike an ordinary part of their cheerless existence.

The first mortal blow to the Podhradie came from fire. On Saturday 17 May 1913, a wedding dinner was being cooked in an attic kitchen at Petöfi (now Beblavého) street 5, and when the family left for the ceremony at the cathedral, the stove was left burning: the blaze, which neither Pressburg's firemen under Franz Sendlein nor the Viennese brigades called in to help could master (fireman Franz Huber died of smoke poisoning), was contained only by evening and finally extinguished on 28 May. About fifty houses burned out — seven on Petöfi street, 23 on Židovská, houses on Zámocká, Mikulášska, Kapucínska, Kozia and Panenská; the Podhradie's oldest building, the 16th-century Edelhof, perished too. The cathedral, both synagogues, Kapitulská street and the House of the Good Shepherd survived as if by miracle — but the Podhradie as a whole ceased to exist even then.

It was bulldozers, not morals, that finished the job: from the late 1950s the houses were torn down — first on sanitary grounds, then for the construction of the [[Most-SNP|SNP Bridge]] — and by the 1970s a whole chapter of the city had vanished from the map together with the Jewish Podhradie and the Neolog synagogue on Rybné námestie. For half a century the slope below the castle stood waste; today Vydrica has been rebuilt as a modern quarter bearing the old name — with a new Floriánska street where the one from the old police reports once ran.
