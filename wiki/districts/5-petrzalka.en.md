# Petržalka (Bratislava V)

Petržalka is Bratislava's largest borough, occupying the right bank of the Danube opposite the historic centre (Bratislava V okres). In the popular imagination it is above all a giant socialist-era panel housing estate — one of the largest in Central Europe — but the place has a pre-panelák biography too: the "propeller" ferry with [[Propeler|Emil Belluš's wharfs]] (1930) crossed to this bank, his Slovak Rowing Club stood here, and schools were built in old pre-war Petržalka and its post-war quarters — among them [[Zakladna-skola-Zaporozska|Vladimír Dedeček's pavilion school on Záporožská street]] (1960s), an example of the "Bratislava type" of school building.

Until 1946 Petržalka was a separate village — the largest in Czechoslovakia (some 1,800 houses and 15,000 residents). The territory's written history begins back in the 13th century (1225) with the later-vanished hamlet of Flocendorf, but the Danube and its branches long made the place unfit for living, and settlement became permanent only in the second half of the 18th century — under the name Engerau (from Ungerau, "Hungarian floodplain"): after a great flood Maria Theresa ordered the Danube regulated, the islets gave way to the main channel, and in 1776 [[Sad-Janka-Krala|Central Europe's oldest public park]] was laid out on the bank. The [[Stary-most|first permanent bridge]] (1890) gave growth its push — factories followed, led by the Matadorka, and the village grew into the country's largest. Petržalka did not join Czechoslovakia at once: in 1918 the border ran along the Danube, and only on the night of 13–14 August 1919, after Vavro Šrobár had negotiated a border adjustment, did the Czechoslovak army cross to the right bank under a moonless sky and take the bridgehead without a single shot. In 1938–1945 Petržalka (together with Devín) was annexed by the Third Reich, and the border again ran right along the Danube. The border tension of the 1930s is recalled by the unique line of [[Petrzalske-bunkre|bunkers B-S 1–15]], and interwar social policy by [[Pavlacovy-dom|Quastler's gallery-access house on Röntgenova]].

New Petržalka began with an unfulfilled dream: in 1966 the city and the International Union of Architects announced Czechoslovakia's largest urban-planning competition — over 80 teams from 19 countries, five third prizes, among them an American proposal for a "Venice on the Danube" with widened river branches and island development. After August 1968 the cooperation with the West was cut off; the project was done by Bratislava's Stavoprojekt (Jozef Chovanec and Stanislav Talaš), and little of the competition ideas survived. The foundation stone was laid on 2 April 1973 on [[Park-obeti-Hraniciarov|Border Guards' Square]], the first panel block on Romanova street was accepted on 3 August 1977; construction proceeded in stages Háje → Lúky → Dvory (the old village was demolished last — 99% of the old buildings fell to the panels), and by 1985 there stood 34,000 flats for 100,000 residents. The planned linear centre with a metro never materialized — a tram line now runs along its route. More than twenty streets of the district are named after Slovakia's castles — Budatínska, Vyšehradská and others. Today Petržalka means 114,000 residents (2021) and over 42,000 flats, Danube branches and meadows along its edges, and a gradual reassessment of the panelák heritage.

## Coat of Arms

![Coat of arms of Petržalka](/assets/erb-5-petrzalka.png)

In a silver field, six trees — five small green ones and one large with a golden trunk — apparently lindens reflected in the surface of the Danube. The motif goes back to Petržalka's seal of 1832 and is probably linked to Central Europe's oldest public park, [[Sad-Janka-Krala|Janko Kráľ Park]]; the coat of arms received its present form in November 1993.

## Objects

- [[Sad-Janka-Krala|Janko Kráľ Park]] — Central Europe's first public park (1775–1776)
- [[Petrzalske-bunkre|The Petržalka bunkers B-S 1–15]] — the First Republic's fortress line, museums in B-S 4, 6 and 8
- [[Pavlacovy-dom|The gallery-access house on Röntgenova]] — Desider Quastler's social housing (1931)
- [[Park-obeti-Hraniciarov|Park of the Border's Victims]] — the estate's first square and the memory of the [[iron-curtain|Iron Curtain]]
- [[Lodenica-Dunajcik|The Dunajčík boathouse]] — Slovakia's oldest paddling club (1924)
- [[Obchodny-dom-Ovsiste|The "Little Prior" in Ovsište]] — from socialist showcase via "House of Horror" to the Bratislavský centre
- [[Zakladna-skola-Zaporozska|Pavilion school on Záporožská (ZŠ Záporožská)]] — the "Bratislava type" school, Vladimír Dedeček
- [[Most-SNP|SNP Bridge and the UFO (Most SNP)]] — the city's calling card and best lookout
- [[Posolstvo-mieru|Message of Peace]] — Pavol Tóth's bronze woman with a dove (1982)
- [[Holubica-mieru|Dove of Peace (Holubica mieru)]] — Karol Lacko's 18-metre steel bird at the southern gateway
- [[Mural-Mier|The "Peace" mural (Mier)]] — the panel district's last monumental painting, saved by its residents

The 1980s "peace" series did not end there: on Námestie hraničiarov stands Ján Ilavský's Peace Song (Mierová pieseň, 1983), and at the entrance of the health school on Strečnianska — Juraj Deák's stone mosaic Peace for Children (Mier deťom). Fountains, though, are almost absent in Petržalka — the estate was built when the "fountain era" of 1965–1989 (the law on a percentage for art in construction) was already fading, and the water features planned with the unrealized linear centre never appeared; of Vladimír Havrilla's fountain "Man and the Universe" only the Zen Girl statue between Turnianska and Krásnohorská remains.
