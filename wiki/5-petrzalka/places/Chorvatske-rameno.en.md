# Croatian Arm (Chorvátske rameno)

The Croatian Arm (Chorvátske rameno), also called the Croatian Canal, is Petržalka's water axis: just over five kilometres (5,138 m) of canal dug along the bed of a real Danube arm, from which it took its name. Locals simply call it "the Canal"; older names are Engerauer Arm, Carlburger Arm and Rusovce Arm. In the Middle Ages it was, after the Pečniansky arm, the mightiest meander of the right bank: it wound across the ground of today's housing estate and separated several islands.

![Croatian Arm (Chorvátske rameno)](/assets/chorvatske-rameno.jpg)

The arm was finally cut off from the river during the building of the right-bank dyke in 1950–1955, by an underground clay-concrete screen sunk 12–16 metres deep into impermeable clay subsoil. The canal was conceived as a drainage and protective channel: it was to intercept seepage when the Danube ran high. That function has never been tested — no such floods have occurred since the arm was built, and the deepening of the Danube bed lowered the groundwater so far that most of the canal was almost dry. A continuous water surface appeared only after the Hrušov reservoir was filled in 1993. There is practically no current; depth varies along its length and is adequate only in the opening stretch, while in the lower reaches the water overheats in summer, harming the oxygen regime. Three weir structures divide the levels.

Biologically, though, the place turned out rich. A sanitary report of July 1993 — six months after the Danube was dammed — counted zooplankton typical of still water, water snails, dragonflies, moorhens and coots, some seventeen swans and thirty carp, green frogs and fire-bellied toads, and young pike. Ten species of amphibian live here today (toads, fire-bellied toads, frogs, tree frogs, the smooth and the Danube newt), with kingfishers and two species of nightingale in the canopy; a soft willow-poplar meadow survives in the lower part, with poplars, ashes, willows, hawthorn and summer snowflake, while herons, little bitterns, ducks, a falcon, long-eared owl, pygmy owl, oriole, woodpeckers, bats, grass snakes, tree frogs and slow-worms keep to the gravel pit. Specialists and residents press ever harder for the area to be declared protected.
