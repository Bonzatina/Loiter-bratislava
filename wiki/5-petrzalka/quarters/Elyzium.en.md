# Elysium (Elýzium)

Elysium (Elýzium) is a vanished quarter of old Petržalka by the [[Stary-most|Old Bridge]], on the right bank opposite today's Eurovea: the poorest part of the city with the poetic and bitterly ironic name of the "abode of blessed souls". Almost nothing remains of the quarter, but its story is a synopsis of how Bratislava-beyond-the-river changed.

The name is older than the settlement itself: first recorded in 1837, it goes back to the Napoleonic wars — during the French siege of the city in 1809 a military cemetery arose here for the French soldiers fallen in the fighting on the southern bank (the same battles in which [[Bianchi-Vinzenz|General Bianchi]] held the defence). The cemetery fell derelict after the troops left; a stone monument renewed on its site in 1922 stood until the bombing of Bratislava in 1944.

The quarter itself was born after 1918, when young Czechoslovakia expropriated the Pálffy estates and the empty bank began to be settled: rural poor from all over Slovakia moved into makeshift houses on Klokočova (where the [[Lodenica-Dunajcik|Dunajčík boathouse]] stands today) and Kočánkova streets — without electricity or sewerage, under constant threat of Danube floods. Next door worked the city's beloved Lido baths: leisure and poverty stood side by side, two faces of one Petržalka. After the 1938 annexation, Nazi propaganda noticed Elysium: Hitler personally visited the settlement during his trip to Engerau, and the Reich press dubbed it the "Hungerkolonie von Engerau" — Petržalka's hunger colony — using photographs of ragged residents to claim Czechoslovakia had not cared for its citizens. The quarter lived the war under the new order — German-only schools, men conscripted into the Wehrmacht, and the Pionier Lager military camp that survived into the 1960s (garden allotments followed).

After the war Elysium quietly melted away — houses disappeared, streets grew over — and in the early 1980s it was finally demolished, together with the Lido baths. Today a modern embankment occupies the site, and few suspect that beneath it once pulsed the life of workers, fishermen and families who believed in a better future.
