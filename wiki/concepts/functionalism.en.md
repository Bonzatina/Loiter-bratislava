# Functionalism

Functionalism was the leading current of interwar Czechoslovak architecture: form follows function, a load-bearing reinforced-concrete skeleton frees the plan and the facade, and the structural elements themselves — ribbon windows, flat roofs, clean volumes — serve as ornament. For Bratislava the style coincided with a turning point: after 1918 provincial Pressburg became the capital of Slovakia within the First Czechoslovak Republic, and the city urgently needed a modern metropolitan centre.

The most striking cluster of functionalism is SNP Square (Námestie SNP) and its surroundings. The [[Obchodny-dom-Dunaj|Dunaj department store (Brouk a Babka)]] (1936) is a textbook example: skeleton frame, free plan, continuous ribbon glazing in stainless steel, neon illumination, built in five months. Next to it stands [[Manderlak|Manderlák]] (1935), Slovakia's first high-rise, where a functionalist structure serves an almost American ambition of its client. A softer variant is [[Lutherov-dom|Luther House]] (1931–1932), where a modern residential complex is dressed in traditional motifs with reliefs by [[Rigele-Alojz|Alojz Rigele]].

These buildings are linked to [[Ludwig-Christian|Christian Ludwig]] — a characteristic representative of the Bratislava version of the style: local architects were rarely dogmatists and readily mixed functionalist technique with context and the conservative tastes of their clients. It was precisely this "impurity" that long kept them in the shadow of their Prague and Brno colleagues. The second great name is [[Bellus-Emil|Emil Belluš]]: from the almost weightless [[Propeler|ferry wharfs of the Propeler]] (1930) through the red-ceramic [[Druzstevne-domy|Coop Houses]] to the travertine [[Narodna-banka-ceskoslovenska|National Bank]] (1938), where functionalism already turns towards Novecento monumentality. Both were preceded by [[Tvarozek-Juraj|Juraj Tvarožek]], whose [[Metropol|Metropol]] (1927–1928) counts among the first important works of Slovak functionalism — conservatively minded ones, with a regular grid of stone-framed windows and Art Deco in the cinema interior.
