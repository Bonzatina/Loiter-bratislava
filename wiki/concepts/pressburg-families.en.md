# Pressburg Families

Old Bratislava was a city of families — German, Hungarian, Slovak and Jewish merchant and artisan dynasties who from 1800 to 1950 built its economy, culture and charity, and in the 20th century were almost entirely "replaced" by new elites. This recurring theme of the city is explored by the Bratislava City Museum's exhibition "Rodinné skutočnosti" in the Apponyi Palace ([[Muzeum-mesta-Bratislavy|MMB]]) — ten family portraits, each a survival strategy under changing regimes.

Merchants and entrepreneurs: the **Scherz** family, with their colonial-goods shop At the Blue Ball, rose to the noble predicate de Vaszója and left for the landed gentry; the **Palugyay** family built an empire of hotels and wine trade with the title of imperial-royal purveyors — their wine was drunk at monarchs' courts and on ocean liners, until the monarchy's collapse and the 1930s crisis ruined the firm; the widows of three generations of the **Hubert** family ran the family construction business themselves and created the legendary sparkling-wine brand — after 1945 the family lost everything, but the Hubert brand lives on (production moved to Sereď); the **Mader-Weisz** family traded in ironware from the 17th century and grew into industrialists; the **Wimmer** family invested in orphanages and people's kitchens.

Other lines show integration and its price: the **Munker-Haeberle** family went in a century from shopkeepers to officers and doctors, shifting language from German to Hungarian and loyalty to Czechoslovak; the noble **Wittmann-Scheidlin** family in the de Pauli palace painted and photographed "Pressburg cosiness"; the musical **Albrecht** family (Alexander and Ján) stayed in the city through every regime, keeping home music-making on Kapitulská as a thread of cultural continuity; the Orthodox Jewish **Sonnenfeld-Suchý** family went from a shop in the Podhradie to aryanized property and the deaths of three relatives in Auschwitz and Ebensee — their house on Kapucínska was demolished in 1972 for a tram line. Together these stories explain the city's deepest fault line: why "exchanged" Bratislava remembers its own builders so poorly — a theme that echoes in the fates of [[Ludwig-Christian|Christian Ludwig]] and [[Rigele-Alojz|Alojz Rigele]].
