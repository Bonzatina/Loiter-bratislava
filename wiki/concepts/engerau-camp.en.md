# The Engerau Camp

From December 1944 to March 1945 a camp for Hungarian Jews operated in [[5-petrzalka|Petržalka]] — then Engerau, annexed by the Third Reich along with [[4-devin|Devín]]. Hundreds died there in four months, and the trials of its guards became the largest complex of Nazi-crime proceedings in Austrian jurisdiction. In Petržalka itself almost nothing recalls it.

![The memorial plaque on the wall of Leberfinger (2017)](/assets/pamatna-tabula-engerau.jpg)

The reason the camp arose here was the "South-Eastern Wall" (Südostwall), the defensive line the Reich began building in the second half of 1944 against the advancing Red Army. The "Lower Danube line" ran from Pressburg to Kőszeg, and the northernmost fortress construction site of the whole wall was Engerau: Bratislava, together with Budapest, was to be "the last invincible rock before Vienna". The order to build positions in Slovakia was issued on 26 November 1944, work began in December, and on 14 December Bratislava was declared a fortress. Between Engerau and Kőszeg, from November 1944 to the end of March 1945, twenty labour camps operated, where some 35,000 Hungarian forced labourers dug trenches.

On 3 December 1944 about two thousand Hungarian Jews arrived from Budapest at Engerau station in closed wagons — men between fifteen and seventy-five, who had previously served "military labour service" in the Hungarian army. At the end of November the Arrow Cross government handed them to Eichmann's Sonderkommando, and on 2–3 December to the SS. The German construction management "Engerau sub-section" sat in Berg in Austria, formed the work columns and ordered the wearing of the yellow star.

The camp was not one object behind one fence — and that is its chief local peculiarity. It consisted of six sub-camps of 250–300 people each, scattered across Petržalka: Auliesel in Starý háj in a former dairy, three by today's [[Incheba|Incheba]] (Fürst in a former timber firm, Schinawek named after the firm, and a sick bay on the premises of the Harsch company), one on Wiesengasse (today's Jaroslavova), one on Bahnhofstrasse (Nádražná) and one at the [[Leberfinger|Leberfinger]] inn. Prisoners lived in cellars, attics, stables and haylofts of private houses and factories — that is, not hidden from local eyes. The camp was guarded not by the SS but by SA men, mostly from Vienna, and by NSDAP "political leaders"; part of the guard was assigned "for special employment" — that is, for killing. People died daily of hunger, cold and exhaustion; others were killed "while attempting to escape".

On 29 March 1945, Maundy Thursday, the camp was evacuated on foot — twenty kilometres to Bad Deutsch-Altenburg via Wolfsthal and Hainburg. The guards drank wine before setting out, and the road turned into a massacre: about a hundred people were shot for falling behind or stopping. At the spot by Leberfinger, Viennese SA men shot at least thirteen prisoners that day, according to the text of the memorial plaque. At Bad Deutsch-Altenburg the survivors were loaded onto barges and carried seven days up the Danube to Mauthausen, which they reached on 6 April; on the way people were shot and starved. Because Mauthausen was overcrowded they were driven further, to the forest camp of Gunskirchen near Wels, where many more died. The few survivors were freed by American troops in early May 1945.

As early as April 1945 a commission assembled by the Slovak government exhumed the dead and buried them at the [[Pomnik-obetiam-Engerau|Petržalka cemetery]]. The numbers differ between sources: the 2017 plaque speaks of 460 exhumed, including more than a hundred victims of the evacuation march; the inscription on the 1958 monument of 497 resting there; Austrian publications of more than five hundred. Only 51 people could be identified, and then from documents the gravedigger had saved.

The legal echo turned out louder than the memory. Between 1945 and 1954 six "Engerau trials" were held before the Vienna People's Court — the largest complex of Nazi-crime cases in Austrian jurisdiction: more than seventy people were investigated, nine death sentences were carried out, and about thirty per cent of all death sentences for Nazi crimes passed by Austrian courts relate to Engerau. In Petržalka itself the first sign of memory was the monument at the cemetery (1958), the second an exhibition, "Engerau: Petržalka's Forgotten Story" (2015), and the third the memorial plaque on the wall of Leberfinger, unveiled on 29 March 2017 — exactly seventy-two years after the march — by the justice ministers of Slovakia, Austria and Hungary, on the initiative of the Vienna Research Centre for Post-War Justice. Its text runs in four languages and ends: "We shall never forget the suffering of the victims nor the crimes of the perpetrators. Never again!"
